How Florida Freelancers Send Money to Family in Estonia

An independent contractor in Florida getting paid in USD faces a different problem than a company paying a vendor: sending part of a freelance paycheck to parents or siblings in Estonia, without losing a chunk of it to a bank's exchange rate markup.

S

Sreekuttan, SEO at Zil Money

Zil Remit Contributor

To send money to Estonia from Florida as a personal transfer, you need four things: the recipient's IBAN and BIC/SWIFT code, their exact legal name as it appears on the account, a plain personal reference like family support, and confirmation of whether the transfer settles in euros or dollars. Get those right and it's a straightforward task. Get one wrong and the payment can sit in review for days while your family waits to hear it arrived.

Freelance income doesn't land on a predictable schedule. One client pays net-15, another pays on delivery, and whatever comes in that month still has to cover rent, taxes, and the people back home who count on part of it. For a freelancer with family in Estonia, that last part is a personal transfer, not a supplier payment or an invoice.

Guides written for small businesses paying overseas vendors assume purchase orders, tax invoices, and a company bank account on the other end. A freelancer in Tampa or Orlando sending money to a parent in Tallinn is doing something simpler on paper, but easier to get wrong in practice: label the transfer like a business payment instead of a personal one, and it can get flagged for manual review while the family member waits to hear whether it arrived.

Key Insight: According to the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide database, the global average cost of sending money internationally was 6.36 percent in the third quarter of 2025, the most recent data published as of this writing. Broken out by channel, bank transfers averaged 14.99 percent that same quarter, while digital providers averaged 4.59 percent. That gap is mostly hidden inside the exchange rate rather than shown as a separate fee line, which is why two providers can each advertise a low upfront fee and still hand a recipient very different final amounts.

Why This Corridor Looks Different From a Typical Business Payment Guide

Most Estonia-focused guides for US businesses (including Zil Remit's own guide to paying Estonian vendors) are written around software vendors, IT consultants, and B2B partnerships, because Estonia has built a reputation as one of Europe's most digital-first economies, home to well over 100,000 e-Residency holders who run companies remotely from outside the country. That context is real, but it describes a different sender than a Florida freelancer sending part of a paycheck to a parent or sibling.

A personal remittance has a simpler goal: get the full amount to a family member's Estonian bank account, in a currency they can actually spend, without the sender needing a business account, a tax invoice, or wording written for a trade transaction. The mechanics overlap with a business payment, but the paperwork and the reason for sending do not.

What a Bank Charges to Send Money to Estonia

A traditional bank international payment commonly carries an upfront fee in the $25 to $50 range, plus an exchange rate markup on top of the mid-market rate. That markup is where the real cost hides. Per the World Bank's own channel breakdown, bank transfers run nearly three times the cost of digital providers on average, once the exchange rate spread is counted alongside the fee.

For a freelancer sending a set amount every month, that gap adds up over a year in a way a single business payment does not. It is worth checking what a provider's exchange rate actually is against the mid-market rate at the moment of sending, not just what fee is advertised on the homepage.

Why the Delivered Amount Is What Actually Matters

Say a freelancer sends $500 a month to a parent in Tallinn. The fee and the exchange rate together decide what actually lands in euros, not the advertised fee by itself. A quote close to the mid-market rate with a modest, disclosed fee can put noticeably more into the recipient's account than a bank quote with a wider spread, even when the sender-side fee looks similar on paper. The only way to know for a specific transfer is to compare the euro amount the recipient will receive, side by side, before confirming.

Personal Remittance vs. Paying a Business: Why the Details Differ

Estonia is part of the eurozone, so a family member there receives euros. That isn't true of every country nearby: Sweden, Norway, and Denmark still use their own currencies, while Estonia's actual land neighbors, Latvia, Lithuania, and Finland, are euro countries too. To send a personal payment, a Florida freelancer needs the recipient's IBAN (International Bank Account Number) and the bank's BIC or SWIFT code, the exact legal name on the account, and a short reference describing why you're sending it, such as family support, rather than wording meant for an invoice or a commercial payment.

Recipient IBAN and BIC/SWIFT code

Estonian IBANs start with "EE" followed by 18 digits, 20 characters total. Ask the recipient to send this directly from their banking app or a recent statement, not from memory. A random typo is usually caught by the IBAN's built-in check digits, but a valid IBAN for the wrong account, a sibling's account instead of a parent's, for example, will not be caught, and that transfer can be very hard to recover.

Exact legal name on the account

Match the spelling on the bank account, not on a passport or a text message.

A reference note that fits a personal transfer

Use a plain description like family support or personal gift, not language written for a business invoice. Payment platforms screen this field for anything that looks like a commercial transaction, and a mismatch here is one of the more common reasons a personal transfer gets held for a manual check.

Settlement currency

Confirm whether the transfer arrives as euros or US dollars. A euro-settled payment usually lands ready to spend, while a dollar-settled one may need a second conversion on the recipient's end, which adds a cost that neither side sees until after it happens.

Step-by-Step: Sending Money From Florida to Family in Estonia

1

Confirm four details in writing

The IBAN, the BIC/SWIFT code, the exact legal name on the account, and whether they want euros or dollars. A screenshot from their banking app beats a number recited over a phone call.

2

Check the exchange rate against the mid-market rate before confirming

A quick search for "USD to EUR mid-market rate" gives a baseline to compare a provider's quoted rate against.

3

Choose a personal purpose note, not a business one

Family support or personal gift keeps the transfer categorized correctly on both ends.

4

Confirm the amount that will actually be delivered

Look at the number the recipient will receive after conversion, not just the amount being sent.

5

Save the recipient's details for next time

Reuse the verified details instead of re-entering them from a text thread every month.

6

Keep a simple record for your own filing

If personal transfers to family become a regular part of a freelancer's monthly budget, a basic log of dates and amounts makes tax season easier. A tax preparer can confirm if any specific transfer needs to be reported.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a Florida business account to send personal money to Estonia?

No. A personal international payment to family does not require a business account. It uses the sender's own account and the recipient's personal IBAN, separate from any business or supplier payment workflow.

What is the difference between sending money to family and paying a business in Estonia?

A personal transfer uses a plain reference like family support and the recipient's personal bank details. A business payment usually requires an invoice reference and wording tied to a commercial transaction, since it's paying for goods or services rather than sending a gift.

Does the recipient in Estonia receive euros or dollars?

It depends on the transfer settings and the recipient's bank. Since Estonia uses the euro, confirming the transfer settles in euros usually means the money is ready to spend without a second conversion on the recipient's side.
