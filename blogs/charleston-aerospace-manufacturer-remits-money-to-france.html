An aerospace-parts manufacturer in Charleston, South Carolina, and a tooling or machining supplier outside Toulouse run this cycle on every purchase order. The supplier machines the jigs, fixtures, or components, ships them with a certificate of conformity, and sends a commercial invoice in euros. The Charleston team's job is simple to state and easy to get wrong: get the right amount to the right account, without a customs mismatch or a paperwork gap that slows down the next order.

This Is a Goods Payment on a Purchase Order, Not a Generic France Guide

A goods payment carries paperwork a personal transfer or a services invoice never touches: a commercial invoice matched to a purchase order, an HS code your customs broker already filed, a certificate of conformity tied to the shipment, and an INCOTERM that decides who paid for freight. Missing any one of those slows the payment down, or creates a mismatch a bank flags for manual review.

If a French supplier's invoice unexpectedly shows VAT on what should be a zero-rated export sale, ask for a corrected invoice before paying (zero-rating depends on the supplier keeping proof the goods left the EU, such as a customs export declaration). A credit note and reissue after the fact is slower. This is general information, not tax advice, so confirm any unusual case with your accountant.

What Your Toulouse Supplier Needs From You Before You Remit Payment

Collect these details in writing, directly from the commercial invoice and the shipment paperwork, not read off a call or pasted from an email thread:

Full legal entity name, matched to their RIB or a bank confirmation letter

Use the name exactly as it appears on the commercial invoice, and check it against the supplier's RIB (Relevé d'Identité Bancaire, the standard French document showing an account's IBAN, BIC, and legal holder name) or a bank confirmation letter, not the certificate of conformity, which covers part quality, not banking details. A mismatch between the invoice name and the IBAN holder is one of the most common reasons a business payment to France gets held for review.

IBAN and BIC/SWIFT code

Take both fields directly from the invoice or the RIB, never retyped from a previous order or a chat message. A copy-paste error in either one is the single most common reason a first payment to a new French supplier gets returned.

The final commercial invoice, not the proforma

The proforma set up the purchase order and any early customs paperwork. The commercial invoice reflects what actually shipped, including any change to quantity or line items, and is the document your payment, your customs broker's entry, and your own accounting records should all point back to.

Confirmation the HS code matches your broker's filing

The payment itself does not carry a customs code, but the invoice line items should match what your customs broker already filed for the shipment. Catching a mismatch here before you pay is a quick fix. Catching it after customs flags the entry is not.

Euros or Dollars: Which Currency Should You Send?

A French tooling or machining supplier almost always invoices in euros. Sending dollars against a euro invoice hands the conversion decision to the receiving bank instead of showing it to you first, which is one way a payment can quietly land short of the invoiced total. A SWIFT payment can also pass through one or more correspondent banks along the route, and each one can deduct a handling fee before the funds reach your supplier, separate from the exchange rate itself.

  Send in Euros Send in US Dollars
Matches the commercial invoice Usually yes, if the invoice was written in euros Only if the invoice specifically states dollars
Who sees the exchange rate first You, before you confirm the payment The supplier's bank, after the payment arrives
Best for A euro-denominated purchase order, the standard case for a French supplier A one-off case where the supplier has specifically requested dollars

Common Questions

What does my Toulouse tooling supplier need from me before I remit the payment?

Get their full legal entity name exactly as it appears on the commercial invoice, their IBAN, their bank's BIC or SWIFT code, and the invoice number, in writing. Match the entity name against their RIB (Relevé d'Identité Bancaire) or a bank confirmation letter before you send anything, not the certificate of conformity, which is a quality document, not a banking one.

Should I remit the payment in euros or dollars?

Pay in the currency the commercial invoice is written in, usually euros. Sending dollars against a euro invoice leaves the conversion math to the receiving bank instead of showing it to you first, which is how a payment can land short.

Should this invoice be paid against the proforma or the final commercial invoice?

The final commercial invoice, not the earlier proforma. The commercial invoice reflects what actually shipped, including any changes to quantity or line items, and is the document your payment and your customs broker should both be matched against.

Will French VAT apply to this payment?

Generally no. A sale of goods that physically leaves France for export to a US buyer is typically zero-rated for French VAT once the supplier keeps proof of export on file, so a correctly issued invoice should not add French tax to a US export sale.

Does this payment need to reference a customs or HS code?

The payment itself does not carry the HS code, but the commercial invoice and your customs broker's entry documents should already match on it. Confirm the HS code lines up with what your broker filed before you release payment.

Do INCOTERMS on the purchase order affect how I remit the payment?

INCOTERMS like FCA or DAP determine who arranges and pays for freight and when risk transfers, not how you remit the invoice. Insurance is a separate contractual matter unless the PO specifically uses a term like CIF or CIP. Check the PO's INCOTERM against what the invoice bills you for, so you don't pay twice for freight already included.

Do I need a W-8BEN-E on file for a French tooling supplier?

A W-8BEN-E generally applies to payments the IRS treats as US-source income to a foreign entity, such as services or royalties. A straightforward purchase of goods manufactured and shipped from France usually is not the kind of payment that triggers this form. Confirm with your accountant if your relationship with the supplier includes any services or licensing beyond the tooling purchase itself.

What is the biggest reason a first payment to a French supplier gets held up?

A mismatch between the entity name on the IBAN and the entity name on the commercial invoice or the supplier's RIB. Banks on both ends flag that kind of mismatch for manual review, and it also mirrors a common payment-fraud pattern.

What if the payment is delayed or something looks wrong after I send it?

Keep the payment confirmation and the invoice reference on hand. Most delays trace back to a mismatched name or account detail caught in manual review.
